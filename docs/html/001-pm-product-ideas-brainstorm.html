Summary

This doc lists about 60 product ideas that DepthMark can build and sell from the data it already has. The ideas cover 11 customer groups, from odds analysts and scouts to media, fans, AI builders, and sponsors. Most ideas sit on four shared engines, so the real choice is which engine to build first. We recommend starting with the narrative engine (Story Feed and the ideas around it), because it is low risk and almost ready. In parallel, we recommend one SQL check that shows whether pre-match signal rates predict anything. That check decides whether the betting ideas live or die. We need a decision on which path to validate first.

Two deciding points#

  1. Most signals are descriptive and post-match, not predictive. For example, referee_strictness says "the first yellow card came in minutes 1 to 5". For betting and prediction products, these signals must be turned into pre-match occurrence rates. For example: "how many times did this team or this referee have this signal in the last 10 matches". Content and scouting products can already use the signals as they are today.
  2. Data usage rights. Raw data, and even source-owned metrics (such as fotmob_rating), cannot safely be sold. Outputs that are DepthMark's own derived insight (signals, narratives, scores, backtests) are easier to defend.

04Recommendation#

  • Fastest path to validation: #1 (Story Feed). Low risk, almost ready, and testable within a few weeks with one test channel or page. #7 (Predict the Story) and #11 (auto match reports) reuse the same engine (E1), so they can follow quickly.
  • Biggest bet: #2 (Backtest Lab). Before building anything, run a simple SQL analysis on ClickHouse. It checks whether pre-match signal rates correlate with results or odds. If they do not, #2, #4, and #9 are dropped.
  • Small but immediate revenue: #3, on the condition that the target is replaced with a non-proprietary metric. #6 (fixture mapping API) is a second candidate, because the work already runs every day.
  • Build engines, not products: build E1 (narrative engine) first, because most quick wins sit on it. Build E2 (pre-match rate layer) only after the correlation check passes.

08Idea catalog#

First round: three perspectives (Product Trio)#

Product Manager (business value)

  1. Pre-match report for odds markets: combine team_form and signal rates with odds to find markets that are probably mispriced. Delivered as a newsletter subscription or a channel.
  2. Selling training datasets: for bootcamps, data science courses, and instructors. The package is a dataset + catalog + solved notebook.
  3. Story Feed for media outlets: an API or feed that, after each match, delivers narrative labels such as "Save Fest", "Last Gasp", and "Blood and Thunder", together with the supporting stats.
  4. Scouting shortlist for lower-league clubs and agents: based on player scenarios (young_gun, line_breaker, progressive_powerhouse).
  5. Card, foul, and referee market intelligence: 60 discipline signals plus referee data, aimed at card and foul markets, which are less efficient.

Product Designer (user experience)

  1. Shareable "match story" cards: ready-made images for Instagram and X, with the narrative label, momentum, and key stats.
  2. Signal Explorer: a web app to browse the 344 signals, with filters for league, team, and date range. A "pattern search engine" for football.
  3. "Player DNA" profile: scenario badges on each player's page (Metronome, Black Hole, …). Gamified and attractive to fantasy users.
  4. Telegram bot to follow a team: narrative and stats after each match, trends before each match. The Persian-language football content market is large.
  5. Fantasy assistant: player suggestions based on scenario consistency and form.

Software Engineer (data and technical leverage)

  1. Signal × odds backtest lab: for each signal or combination of signals, it calculates the historical ROI on Oddspedia markets. The oddspedia_match_resolution link already exists, and this is a rare advantage.
  2. "Ask DepthMark": natural-language questions over Silver/Gold using an LLM and text-to-SQL, with the signal catalog as context.
  3. Feature Store for modelers: versioned pre-match features (rolling signal rates, form, xG) for quants and researchers.
  4. Live (in-play) pattern detection: run signals on the Live snapshot and momentum, and send alerts during the match.
  5. Embed widgets for websites and blogs: signal badges and match cards as white-label widgets.

09Priority idea write-ups#

1. Story Feed and match story cards#

Description: after each match, the narrative labels produced by the signals are delivered together with the supporting stats. Delivery is as an API, a feed, or ready-made images for football page admins, media outlets, and content creators.

Why: the signals already have narrative names and already run. The output is derived insight, not raw data, so it is the lowest-risk legal path. The football content market (especially the Persian-language market) is large, and creators are always looking for a "story angle".

Assumptions to test:

  • Content creators will pay for a story angle, and do not find the free FotMob stats enough on their own.
  • The current delay (the pipeline runs on completed dates) is acceptable, or the Live path needs to be ready sooner.
  • The audience finds the labels "right" and interesting, not just statistical.

2. Signal × odds backtest lab#

Description: a tool that shows the historical ROI and hit rate you would have had if you had acted on a market based on the pre-match rate of one signal (or a combination of signals).

Why: connecting FotMob ↔ Oddspedia in one clean warehouse is a rare technical advantage. The customers (odds analysts and quants) can pay a lot. This idea has the highest potential value.

Assumptions to test:

  • After they are turned into pre-match features, the signals have predictive power (the riskiest assumption).
  • Historical odds coverage and the event match rate are high enough for a meaningful backtest.
  • Commercial use of scraped odds is allowed. Selling this tool in markets where betting is illegal is also forbidden or high-risk, so the target market must be outside those jurisdictions.

3. Training datasets and kits#

Description: a package of dataset + catalog + solved notebook + exercises for data science courses, built from the Silver tables.

Why: Silver data (such as silver.player_match_stat) is already clean and conformed. So this is the cheapest way to get a first revenue signal.

Assumptions to test:

  • The current target (fotmob_rating) is a FotMob-owned metric. It probably needs to be replaced with a DepthMark-owned score or another target (for example match result or goals).
  • Instructors and bootcamps will pay for a clean football dataset, even though free competitors exist (StatsBomb Open Data and Kaggle).

4. Card and referee market intelligence#

Description: referee strictness profiles and team and competition discipline patterns, for card and foul markets.

Why: niche markets are less efficient and have less data competition. DepthMark has 60 discipline signals and referee data.

Assumptions to test:

  • oddspedia_bronze.market actually covers card and foul markets (not checked yet).
  • The referee name in silver.match (referee_name) is filled in for the target leagues.
  • Referee strictness is stable over time and can be predicted.

5. Player DNA profile and scouting shortlist#

Description: each player's scenario badges and how consistent they are over the season, plus a "similar players" filter. Aimed at small clubs, agents, and fantasy users.

Why: the player scenarios are ready, and the product layer is mostly UI. A free version to attract an audience and a paid version for scouts are both possible.

Assumptions to test:

  • Lower-league coverage is deep enough. Scouting value sits exactly where Wyscout is expensive.
  • Scouts trust FotMob-derived stats compared with professional tools.
  • Fantasy users will pay for badges, not just for form.

6. Fixture ID mapping API#

Description: an API that answers "which FotMob match is this Oddspedia event?" and the reverse, with a confidence score and the rule that matched it.

Why: joining an odds source to a stats source is a known pain for every developer who builds betting models or odds comparison sites. DepthMark already does this every day in silver.oddspedia_match_resolution. The output is a mapping between IDs, not raw stats, so the legal risk is lower than selling data.

Assumptions to test:

  • The match rate and confidence are high enough to sell (measure the share of events with a confident match).
  • Developers will pay for mapping rather than build their own fuzzy matching.
  • Publishing IDs from both sources is allowed under their terms.

7. Predict the Story (free game)#

Description: before each match, players pick which narrative labels the match will get, such as "Goal Fest", "Blood and Thunder", or "Save Fest". After the match, the real labels decide the points. Leagues between friends and weekly leaderboards keep people coming back.

Why: it is a new kind of prediction game that only DepthMark can score, because only DepthMark has the labels. It is free to play, so no gambling licence is needed. It also feeds the Story Feed audience and gives sponsors a clear slot.

Assumptions to test:

  • Fans understand the labels without a long explanation.
  • The labels are not so rare or so common that guessing becomes boring (check the activation rate of each label).
  • Sponsors will pay for named labels or weekly prizes.

8. MCP server for football data#

Description: a tool server that AI assistants and AI apps can call to ask for signals, scenarios, player fingerprints, and pre-match rates.

Why: many people now build football assistants on top of LLMs. They need clean, structured football facts with clear meaning. The signal catalog in MongoDB already gives each signal a name, description, and grain, which is exactly what an AI tool needs.

Assumptions to test:

  • AI app builders will pay per call rather than scrape free sites.
  • The answers stay derived insight, not a pipe of raw source data.
  • Usage can be limited and metered without heavy new infrastructure.

9. Goal timing and corner markets#

Description: pre-match profiles of when each team scores and concedes, and how many corners each team tends to win and give away.

Why: timing and corner markets get less attention than match result markets. DepthMark has goal times in silver.shot, corners per period in silver.period_stat, and matching signals and scenarios (early_blitz, late_surge_goals, last_gasp, set_piece_dominance).

Assumptions to test:

  • oddspedia_bronze.market has timing and corner markets (check market_name values).
  • Past timing and corner patterns repeat enough to predict.
  • Same legal limits as the backtest lab: legal betting markets only.

10. Fatigue and fixture congestion#

Description: a weekly table of rest days, running load, and late-match collapses, and a "tired legs" warning before each match.

Why: physical data (distance_covered_*, number_of_sprints_*, top_speed_*) is rarely used in public football content. Rest days come straight from match dates. The output feeds media content, fantasy rotation warnings (E4-g), and pre-match reports.

Assumptions to test:

  • Physical fields are filled in for enough leagues (check null rates by league_id).
  • Short rest really links to worse second-half numbers in this data.

11. Auto-written match reports#

Description: a short written report for every match, built by an LLM from the narrative labels and key stats, in several languages.

Why: small media sites cannot cover every league. Labels give the LLM a clear story to tell, so reports are less generic. Persian-language coverage is a gap.

Assumptions to test:

  • Reports are accurate (no made-up facts) when the LLM only sees structured inputs.
  • Editors will publish them with light review.
  • Search engines and readers accept automated reports in this niche.

12. Similar-player finder#

Description: given a player, finds others with a close fingerprint of signals and scenarios, with filters for age, league, and minutes played.

Why: it turns E3 into a direct scouting tool. It is cheaper than professional platforms and focuses on lower leagues where they are expensive.

Assumptions to test:

  • Fingerprints of players in the same role look alike, and different roles look different (a simple sanity check).
  • Enough minutes per player exist in lower leagues to build a stable fingerprint.
